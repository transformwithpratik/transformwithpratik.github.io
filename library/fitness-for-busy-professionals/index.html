Fitness for busy professionals

2 min read · By Pratik Jain

You do not need two hours a day. You need a small amount of the right training, done most weeks, for a long time. Here is the minimum that works and how to protect it from your calendar.

The minimum effective week

  • Strength training 2 or 3 times a week, about 40 to 50 minutes. Full-body sessions built on a squat, a hinge, a push, a pull and a carry or core exercise.
  • Daily movement. The WHO recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus muscle-strengthening on 2 or more days. Brisk walking counts, and any bout counts; there is no 10-minute minimum anymore.
  • Progress slowly. Add a rep or a little weight when every set feels solid. That is how strength builds.

See the exercise library for two weekly templates.

When there is no time at all: exercise snacks

Short bursts of hard effort spread through the day, such as climbing stairs briskly for a minute, have been tested in trials. A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials found that snacks of 5 minutes or less, done at least twice a day, improved fitness in inactive adults. They did not change body fat, blood pressure or cholesterol, so treat them as a fitness top-up on busy days, not a replacement for training.

Making it stick

  1. Put sessions in your calendar like meetings, at the time you are least likely to be interrupted. For most people that is early morning.
  2. Have a short version. If a day blows up, do 20 minutes: two exercises, three sets each. Something beats nothing.
  3. Train where it is easy. A gym on the way to work, or a few dumbbells at home, beats the perfect gym across town.
  4. Plan for travel and deadlines in advance (see Travel fitness).
  5. Track it. Writing down sets and weights shows progress on weeks when the mirror does not.

What to expect

Strength goes up in the first few weeks. Visible changes in the mirror usually take 8 to 12 weeks of consistent training and eating. Missing a session is normal; missing the habit is the problem, so get back on plan at the next scheduled session.

Sources

  1. Bull FC, et al. World Health Organization 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour. Br J Sports Med. 2020;54:1451-1462.
  2. Rodríguez et al. Effect of exercise snacks on fitness and cardiometabolic health in inactive adults: systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials. Br J Sports Med. 2026.
