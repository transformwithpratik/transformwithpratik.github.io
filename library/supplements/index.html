Supplements: what is worth it

2 min read · By Pratik Jain

Supplements are the last few percent. Food, training and sleep do the heavy lifting. That said, a handful have solid evidence and can make life easier. Here is how I rank them.

Worth considering

Creatine monohydrate

The most researched sports supplement. With strength training, 3 to 5 g a day improves strength and helps build muscle, and it is considered safe for healthy adults. Vegetarians tend to have lower creatine stores in muscle, so they may notice a bigger effect. No loading phase is needed; take it at any time of day. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor first.

Whey or plant protein

Not magic, just convenient protein. A scoop typically adds around 20 to 25 g. Useful when you cannot reach your protein target with food, for example on travel days or with a light breakfast. It counts towards your daily total, it does not add to it.

Caffeine

Doses of about 3 to 6 mg per kg of body weight, taken 30 to 60 minutes before training, reliably improve performance. A strong coffee is often enough. Caffeine can cost you sleep even when you fall asleep fine, so keep it away from the evening (see Sleep and recovery).

Test first, then decide

Vitamin D

Low vitamin D is common in Indian studies. Your skin makes the most around midday rather than at sunrise, and smog and sunscreen reduce it. Get a blood test and follow your doctor's dose rather than guessing.

Vitamin B12

A 2026 review estimated that about half of healthy young vegetarian adults in Indian studies are low in B12, and plenty of non-vegetarians are too. A blood test is cheap. Supplement if you are low.

Omega-3 (fish or algae oil)

Reasonable if you never eat fish, but evidence for large health benefits in healthy people is mixed. Ask your doctor if you take blood thinners.

Usually skip

  • Fat burners and detox teas. No good evidence for meaningful fat loss, and some contain stimulants or laxatives.
  • Testosterone boosters. Herbal blends without convincing human evidence.
  • BCAAs. Little point if you already eat enough protein.
  • Apple cider vinegar for weight loss. The best-known trial behind the claim was retracted in 2025.
  • Trendy "glow" supplements such as NAD+ and glutathione: heavily promoted, with little good evidence in healthy people.

Buying safely

Pick products with an FSSAI licence number, a full ingredient list and, ideally, third-party testing (such as Informed Sport or Informed Choice). Avoid anything promising rapid fat loss or muscle gain. If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication or have a health condition, speak to your doctor before starting any supplement.
